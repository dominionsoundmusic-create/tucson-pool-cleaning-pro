## 1. Draining a pool and filter backwash (City of Tucson, Pima County, ADEQ)

### City of Tucson (Department of Transportation and Mobility, Stormwater)
- Sanitary sewer is the preferred route for draining a pool or spa. Discharge goes through the access cap of the PRIVATE property sewer cleanout, using a hose and a small pump, ideally during low-use sewer hours, with the pump controlled so the cleanout does not overflow. "DO NOT use public manholes or cleanouts."
- Backwash: "Backwash water may be discharged onto pool/spa owner's property as long as it can be absorbed into the ground within 24 hours and does not flow onto a neighboring property, street or alley." The city states surface flow of filter backwash off the property is not allowed under state and local discharge permit requirements.
- Backwash "commonly contains elevated levels of chlorine, pathogens and other potential contaminants that should not be released off-site."
- Surface drainage (street, storm drain, wash) of pool water is a last resort, allowed only if there is no access to a private sewer cleanout AND all conditions are met: untreated at least 72 hours (3 days) after the last chlorine addition, pH measured and adjusted to 7 to 8, no acid-washed water, water clear, and no erosion or flow onto neighboring property or sidewalks.
- "Under no circumstances will a pool or spa be allowed to be permanently connected to a stormdrain or wash."
- Enforcement example: the city's 2020 to 2021 AZPDES stormwater annual report notes approximately 25 reports of pool draining or filter backwashing; inspectors directed owners to stop the flow and keep backwash on site or send it to a sanitary sewer cleanout.
- City Stormwater Management Section: (520) 791-4251.
- New construction: City of Tucson 2024 International Swimming Pool and Spa Code amendments require "all new and remodeled exterior installed swimming pools, spas and hot tubs" to be provided with a cover to limit evaporation before final inspection approval.

### Pima County Regional Wastewater Reclamation Department (RWRD)
- "Pima County's Regional Wastewater Reclamation Department has approved swimming pool water discharge into the public sewer (sanitary sewer) collection system, and sanitary sewer discharge is a preferred method to manage this water." (This text appears in the shared City/County discharge guidelines.)
- Same method: private property cleanout cap, hose, small controlled pump, low-use hours, no public manholes or cleanouts. Owner leaves water untreated at least 72 hours after last chlorine; "The owner or service company shall measure pH prior to discharge and adjust it to the acceptable range."
- Pima County's "Protect Our Pipes" page has a section on whether pool water can go in the sewer (text not returned).
- Pima County Department of Environmental Quality is not responsible for private residential pools and spas (per search summary of the guidelines).
- RWRD main line (520) 724-6500; sewer emergency line (520) 724-3400.
- Sources:

### ADEQ
- The AZPDES De Minimis General Permit (DMGP) covers dechlorinated freshwater swimming pool drainage to surface waters as a low-flow, short-duration discharge. Pool drainage must be visually clear, colorless, and free of suspended solids, floating material and debris. The 2021 fact sheet indicates listed pool drainage meeting the criteria is authorized without a separate notice of intent.
- ADEQ is reissuing the DMGP as AZG2026-001 to replace AZG2021-001; public comment ran June 26 to July 27, 2026. Final issuance NOT confirmed.
- ADEQ 1.05 General Permit (aquifer protection) covers swimming pool filter backwash under 1,000 gallons per day, discharged only to injection wells, surface impoundments or leach lines.
- Sources:
  - AZPDES De Minimis General Permit: https://azdeq.gov/AZPDES/DMGP (verified via search summary)
  - Fact Sheet for 2021 De Minimis General Permit: https://static.azdeq.gov/permits/azpdes/dmgp_fs.pdf (verified via search summary)
  - R18-9-B301 / Type 1 general permits: https://legacy.azdeq.gov/environ/water/permits/download/rules/1.03.pdf (verified via search summary)
- Site guidance: for homeowner pages, lead with the City/County rules (sewer cleanout preferred, backwash stays on your lot). ADEQ detail is background only.

## 2. Monsoon, dust storms, climate normals (NWS Tucson)
- Official monsoon season: June 15 to September 30. This fixed definition took effect in 2008 and is shared by the southwestern NWS offices (Tucson, Phoenix, Flagstaff). Before 2008, Tucson's start was the third consecutive day with an average daily dewpoint of 54 F or higher.
  - The Monsoon June 15 to September 30 (NWS Tucson PDF): https://www.weather.gov/media/twc/Monsoon.pdf (verified via search summary)
  - Monsoon Safety, NWS Tucson: https://www.weather.gov/twc/monsoonsafety (verified via search summary)
  - Monsoon Information Page, NWS Tucson: https://www.weather.gov/twc/Monsooninfo (verified via search summary)
- Monsoon rainfall at Tucson: normal 6.06 inches (normal period not stated on page); driest season on record 1.59 inches, wettest 13.84 inches. Source: Monsoon Information Page above.
- Dust storms / haboobs: form when thunderstorm outflow (rain-cooled air) pushes dust ahead of it; more common early in the monsoon but possible any time it is stormy; NWS Tucson notes they most often form in the deserts north and west of Tucson; usually last a few minutes, up to about an hour; visibility can drop to near zero; numerous fatal crashes on Interstate 10. A Dust Storm Warning means visibility of a quarter mile or less. Driving advice: pull fully off the road, turn off all lights, stay put.
  - Monsoon Safety, NWS Tucson: https://www.weather.gov/twc/monsoonsafety (verified via search summary)
- 2026 season note: the NWS Tucson 2026 Monthly and Seasonal Reviews page was summarized as describing a record-early start (June 14) and a wet end in late September. This conflicts with the fixed June 15 start, so do NOT use it on the site without fetching the page. https://www.weather.gov/twc/2026MonthlyReviews
- Tucson climate normals, 1991 to 2020 (Tucson International Airport):
  - Average July high: 100.2 F; average July low: 76.3 F.
  - Days at or above 100 F: 68 per year on average (July about 19).
  - Annual precipitation normal: 10.61 inches.
  - July precipitation normal: about 2.21 inches.
  - Sources: Tucson Monthly and Daily Normals and Records: https://www.weather.gov/twc/TucsonMonthlyNormalExtremes ; Tucson highs of 100+ info: https://www.weather.gov/twc/Tucson100syearly ; annual 10.61 inch figure from an NWS Tucson year-in-review/climate report page (exact page not identified; see https://www.weather.gov/twc/2024MonthlyClimateReports). All verified via search summary. Re-check the July high figure if it will be stated precisely; "about 100 F" is safe.

## 3. Pool evaporation
- Arizona Department of Water Resources (ADWR): "In Phoenix and Tucson, the average evaporation rate is approximately six feet per year," most of it in summer. A standard 16 by 36 foot pool loses four to six feet per year to evaporation; with refilling and backwashing that is roughly like filling the pool once a year.
- ADWR: "90% to 95%" of pool and spa water lost to evaporation can be saved with a pool cover.
  - ADWR Pools and Spas fact sheet: https://www.azwater.gov/sites/default/files/media/Pool&Spas_2015_1.pdf and https://www.azwater.gov/sites/default/files/ADWR's%20Pools%20and%20Spas%20Fact%20Sheet.pdf (verified via search summary)
- University of Arizona Water Resources Research Center (Arroyo, 1995, older data): a 400 square foot pool needed 16,291 gallons a year to refill evaporation losses after rainfall; total operation about 20,286 gallons a year including backwashing and periodic draining. Covers are the prime strategy to reduce pool water use.
  - Water Recreation Makes Big Splash in Arizona: https://wrrc.arizona.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/arroyo_1995_v8_n3_w.pdf (verified via search summary; label as a 1995 study if used)
- City of Tucson code: new and remodeled outdoor pools, spas and hot tubs must have a cover to limit evaporation before final inspection (see section 1 source).
- Tucson Water landscape guidance (irrigation, not pools): water outside 10 a.m. to 6 p.m., when evaporation is highest. https://tucsonaz.gov/water/landscape (verified via search summary)
- No per-day summer figure was found from an official source.

## 5. Natural gas: Southwest Gas
- Southwest Gas serves Tucson and Green Valley through its Southern Arizona division and describes itself as distributing gas in most of central and southern Arizona, including the Tucson metro. It has an LNG storage facility on 31 acres in southeast Tucson backing up supply for more than 280,000 Southern Arizona customers.
  - Contact Us: https://www.swgas.com/en/contact-us ; Southern Arizona LNG Reliability Project: https://www.swgas.com/lng (verified via search summary; contact page metadata was old)
- Gas emergency line: 877-860-6020, available day or night, callers do not need to be customers; also call 911. (An older tariff listed a Southern Arizona emergency line (800) 722-4277; use 877-860-6020, which is on the current Report a Leak page.)
- If you smell gas: leave the area or building immediately and tell others to leave; call 911 and Southwest Gas once you are safely away; do not smoke or use matches or lighters; do not flip electrical switches, thermostats or appliance controls; do not start or stop an engine or use an automatic garage door opener. Signs: rotten-egg or sulfur smell (mercaptan is added because natural gas is odorless), hissing or roaring sound, dirt or water blowing into the air or bubbling.
  - Report a Leak: https://www.swgas.com/en/report-a-leak ; Home and Business Safety: https://www.swgas.com/en/safety ; Natural Gas Safety 2025 (PDF): https://www.swgas.com/1409224058698/Natural-Gas-Safety-2025-EN.pdf (verified via search summary)

## 6. Pima County Health Department: mosquitoes and green pools
- Pima County asks residents to report mosquito breeding problems "(e.g. green pools)" to 520-724-7908. The Vector Control Program uses the same number for information or complaints.
- Pool, housing and vector/mosquito complaints are handled by the Consumer Health and Food Safety division.
- Prevention advice (fall 2025 county newsletter): keep chlorine or bromine concentrations up in pools, or drain pools that will not be used long term.
- West Nile virus: the county has issued releases on West Nile cases and positive mosquito pools in past seasons; the result returned was about 620 days old, so no current-year figure.
  - Mosquitoes, Pima County: https://www.pima.gov/2100/Mosquitoes ; Vector Control Program: https://www.pima.gov/2296/Vector-Control-Program ; Consumer Health and Food Safety Newsletter: https://www.pima.gov/2471/Consumer-Health-Food-Safety-Newsletter (verified via search summary)
- Do not repeat the newsletter's "thin oil layer" tip on the site (it is chemical/DIY advice and not suited to a pool).

## 7. CDC: chemical safety and water balance
- Never mix pool chemicals with each other or with any other substance; "Never mix chlorine products with acid; this could create toxic gases." Recommendations are generally the same for residential and public pools.
- Storage: keep in original labeled containers, dry, locked away from children and animals, below 95 F (35 C) and out of direct sunlight, apart from incompatible products; store liquids low. Read labels each time, wear goggles and gloves, add chemicals to water and never water to chemicals.
  - Pool Chemical Safety: https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/toolkit/pool-chemical-safety.html ; storage poster: https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/media/pdfs/Pool-Chemical-Safety-STORAGE-poster-p.pdf ; use poster: https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/media/pdfs/Pool-Chemical-Safety-USE-poster-p.pdf (verified via search summary)
- Injury scale: CDC reported pool chemical injuries lead to over 4,500 emergency department visits each year (2019 release, 2008 to 2017 data). https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/media/releases/2019/p0515-pool-chemical-injuries.html (verified via search summary, title only)
- Home pool water levels (CDC current home pool page): pH 7.0 to 7.8; free chlorine at least 1 ppm in pools without stabilizer; at least 2 ppm when cyanuric acid (stabilizer) or stabilized chlorine is used. CDC recommends not using cyanuric acid in hot tubs. Older CDC material used pH 7.2 to 7.8; use the current 7.0 to 7.8.
  - Home Pool and Hot Tub Water Treatment and Testing: https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/about/home-pool-and-hot-tub-water-treatment-and-testing.html (verified via search summary)
- Cyanuric acid: CDC notes disinfection takes longer when cyanuric acid is present and that CYA decreases chlorine efficacy against Cryptosporidium. The CDC Model Aquatic Health Code (public pools) limit is 90 ppm or less.
  - Operating and Managing Public Pools: https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/toolkit/operating-public-pools-hot-tubs-and-splash-pads.html ; Effect of Cyanuric Acid on the Inactivation of Cryptosporidium: https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/151609/cdc_151609_DS1.pdf (verified via search summary; label 90 ppm as the public-pool code limit, not a home rule)
