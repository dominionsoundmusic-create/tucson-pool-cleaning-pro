Your pool pump stopped working, the water is still and the return jets are quiet. Before you assume the worst, know that many stoppages come from causes you can check yourself in a few minutes, without tools and without opening anything electrical. This guide gives you that order, shows what each finding means, and marks the point where the job belongs to a professional.

If the checks do not solve it, the pool pump repair page explains the parts that fail, what repairs cost according to published guides, and how to think about repair versus replacement.

Safety before you start. Switch the pump off at its timer or control before you touch the lid or baskets. If you see water pooled around the motor, smell burning or see scorch marks, do not touch the pump or the switch next to it: keep everyone away and call for help. Keep children away from the pool while the water is not circulating, and never leave a child alone near the water. A pool that turns cloudy or green is a drowning hazard because you cannot see the bottom.

Pool pump troubleshooting, in the order to check

Work down the list. Each step tells you what a normal result looks like and where to go if it is not normal. Stop as soon as you find the cause.

  1. Is the pump supposed to be on right now? Look at the timer or automation schedule. After a power outage or a monsoon storm, a clock can lose its time or a schedule can reset, so the pump may simply be running at the wrong hours. If the time is wrong, correct it and see whether the pump starts on schedule.
  2. Has a GFCI or breaker tripped? Check the GFCI outlet or breaker that feeds the pump. A tripped one gets a single reset, no more. Should it trip a second time, leave it off. That is the stop line, and the next step is a phone call. The CPSC also advises testing permanently installed GFCIs at least once a month.
  3. Is there enough water at the skimmer? Angi suggests a water line roughly at the middle of the skimmer opening. Below that, the pump can draw air instead of water. The Arizona Department of Water Resources puts open-water evaporation in Tucson at about six feet a year, most of it in summer, so a level that was fine last week can drop.
  4. Are the skimmer and pump baskets clear? With the pump off, empty the skimmer basket and the basket inside the pump's strainer pot. After a dust storm or a windy day, they can pack full in a matter of hours.
  5. Is the pump lid seated and sealed? Look at the clear lid for cracks and check that its O-ring sits evenly in its groove. Angi lists air leaks, often from loose fittings or cracked O-rings, as the leading reason a pump loses prime.
  6. Are the valves open? Valve handles near the pump control where water comes from and goes. If someone turned one recently, a closed or half-closed valve can starve the pump. Put them back in the positions your pool company set.
  7. What does the filter pressure gauge say? Note the reading with the pump running. A reading well above the normal clean level suggests a dirty filter that is choking flow. A reading near zero with the pump running points to a pump that is not moving water.
  8. Is the motor buried or blocked? Leaves, gravel, stored items or a cover over the motor can trap heat. Clear the area so air can reach the motor's vents.

What you notice and what it usually means

Not every pump that "stopped" is silent. Use the sound and the behavior to narrow it down.

Running a pump without water can overheat it quickly and damage it, Angi warns. If the strainer pot is empty of water and the pump is roaring, turn it off rather than letting it keep trying.

The filter is part of the picture

A pump and filter work as a pair. When the filter clogs, the pump pushes against more resistance, moves less water and runs hotter. That is why the pressure gauge is on the checklist. If the reading has climbed since the last cleaning, the filter likely needs attention, which is routine work for a pool service company. The pool cleaning and maintenance page explains what a regular visit includes.

The stop line: where your checks end

Fine for a homeowner

  • Reading and correcting the timer or automation schedule
  • Resetting a tripped GFCI or breaker one time
  • Checking the water level at the skimmer
  • Emptying both baskets once the pump is switched off
  • Looking at the pump lid and its O-ring for cracks
  • Returning valves to their usual positions
  • Reading the filter pressure gauge
  • Clearing leaves and clutter away from the motor

Leave to a professional

  • Opening the motor, its wiring cover or the control box
  • Testing or replacing a capacitor, which can hold a charge with the power off
  • Anything inside the electrical panel beyond one reset
  • A breaker or GFCI that trips a second time
  • Taking the pump apart to reach the impeller or seal
  • Any work on wiring, a disconnect or a new circuit

Arizona's licensing lines up with this split. The Registrar of Contractors' R-6 license for residential pool service and repair excludes electrical work beyond the first disconnect, so even pool companies hand some electrical jobs to an electrical contractor.

Why Tucson summers stop pumps

The same pump that runs fine in March can start shutting off in July. The National Weather Service records an average July high of about 100 degrees at Tucson, with an average of 68 days a year at or above 100 degrees (1991 to 2020 normals). A motor sitting on a sunny equipment pad has little room to cool if anything is making it strain.

Monsoon season, which the NWS Tucson office defines as June 15 to September 30, adds wind and dust. Storm outflow can drop leaves, dust and seed pods into the pool in minutes, and much of it lands in the baskets. Storms can also cut power, which is one reason a timer may be off afterward. A Tucson pool maintenance schedule that checks baskets more often in summer helps.

If your home is on a Tucson Electric Power time-of-use plan, TEP's summer on-peak hours are 3 to 7 p.m. on weekdays, and it suggests running a pool pump in the morning or at midday. When you reset a timer, that is a sensible window to keep, but check your plan's current hours. Depending on your address, your power may come from Trico Electric Cooperative instead.

Call now if you see any of these

  • A burning smell, smoke or scorch marks at the motor or the switch. Keep away and do not touch anything electrical.
  • Water at the motor or pooled under the pump. Water and electricity together are dangerous.
  • A GFCI or breaker that trips again after one reset.
  • Humming without spinning, or screeching and grinding.
  • A pump that keeps losing prime after you have checked the water level, baskets and lid.

While you wait, leave the pump off, keep children and pets out of the pool area, and if the water is starting to turn, read what to do when the pool turns green. Never add or mix pool chemicals to make up for a pump that is not running.

What to tell the pool company

A short, clear description saves time: what the pump does or does not do, any sound, whether a breaker tripped, what the filter gauge read, roughly how old the pump is, and which checks you already made. Before any work, ask which Arizona ROC license the company holds and note the number.
