Oro Valley: a town that grew up under the Catalinas

Oro Valley became a town on April 15, 1974. Town history documents describe a small starting area, about 2.5 square miles, and a population of nearly 1,200. By the 2020 Census it had 47,070 people. Much of that growth happened in planned communities. One of the biggest is Rancho Vistoso, whose planned area development plan dates to 1987 and covers about 7,626 acres; its first neighborhood is the Del Webb adult community, Sun City Vistoso. Stone Canyon is governed by its own homeowners association with CC&Rs and design guidelines, and La Reserve is another of the town's planned area development districts.

That pattern matters for a pool owner. If your home sits inside an HOA, its rules may cover things like where equipment can go, what a new barrier looks like and how the yard is kept, so read them before you replace equipment or change a wall. To the east, the Santa Catalina Mountains and Pusch Ridge close off the view, and Catalina State Park, about 5,500 acres of foothills, canyons and streams, sits at their base, close to the town's northern neighborhoods.

Oro Valley pool service and the town's water

Oro Valley Water Utility is run by the town. In a 2023 article the town said its drinking water was about 65 percent groundwater and 35 percent Central Arizona Project water, which is Colorado River water brought in by canal and blended with what the wells produce. That blend is the reason hardness moves around. The utility's 2024 annual report, covering 2023, gives a range of 2.1 to 15.8 grains per gallon (36 to 270 parts per million) and says the figure depends on how much CAP water is mixed into a given pressure zone. On the utility's own scale, anything over 180 parts per million counts as very hard.

Hardness is a measure of dissolved calcium and magnesium. The utility says it poses no health risk and no agency regulates it, yet those same minerals build the chalky white band that creeps along pool tile. Every hot week, water evaporates and the minerals stay behind; the Arizona Department of Water Resources puts open water evaporation in the Tucson area at about six feet a year. Each top-off adds a little more calcium. That is why a careful pool technician here checks calcium levels along with the everyday chlorine reading and keeps an eye on the tile. Not every address is on the town system, so check whose name is on your water bill. If it is Oro Valley Water Utility and you want the number for your street, the utility answers that at 520-229-5000.

Draining a pool by Oro Valley's guidelines

Oro Valley's Stormwater Utility publishes its own pool and spa drainage guidelines, and they differ in places from the guidance used elsewhere in the county. Read the town's version, not a neighboring city's, before any water leaves the yard.

Backwash stays home

Filter backwash may not be sent to streets or washes. It may go onto your own property if the ground absorbs it, and it may not run onto a neighbor's lot, the street or an alley.

Street drainage has conditions

Prepared pool water may go to a curbed road, storm drain or wash only if it stays off neighboring property, causes no erosion and is non-toxic, with chlorine below 1 part per million. Unpaved roads, strip-paved roads and alleys are not allowed.

Salt pools go to the sewer

The town says salt water pools must be drained to the sanitary sewer. If you are unsure, the Stormwater Utility takes pool discharge questions at 520-229-4850. Our guide to draining a pool in the Tucson area covers the wider picture.

Barriers, storms and safety in Oro Valley

The town's barrier height is 60 inches

Oro Valley's rule is taller than some nearby codes. The town's pool permit page and its adopted 2024 amendments to the International Swimming Pool and Spa Code require the top of a residential barrier to be at least 60 inches above grade on the outside. The 2024 amendments also set latching hardware at 54 inches or higher and require self-closing, self-latching doors where a door opens onto the pool area. The town's 2019 residential construction handbook says pool equipment must sit at least 48 inches from the barrier or be enclosed on its own, so it cannot be used as a step. If a company suggests moving equipment, ask how the new spot fits that rule. Older town documents show a lower height, and the handbook predates the current amendments, so confirm with permitting before you build.

Monsoon debris

Tucson's National Weather Service office dates the monsoon from June 15 to September 30. Storm outflow can push dust ahead of it, and the weather service notes that dust storms most often form in the deserts north and west of Tucson. After a storm, clear the skimmer and pump baskets and check the water level; heavy debris and fresh rain both throw off the water balance.

  • Baskets. After wind or rain, lift out and clear both the skimmer basket and the one in front of the pump.
  • Filter gauge. Jot down what the gauge reads; a number well above normal is worth mentioning when you call.
  • Timer. Outages can wipe a clock, so confirm the pump still runs at the hours you set.
  • Breaker once. One reset of a tripped GFCI or pool breaker is fine. A second trip means it stays off until a professional looks.
  • Chemicals. Pool chemicals never get combined. Keep each in its original container, separated, dry, shaded and locked where children cannot reach, and do what the label says.

Smell gas near a pool heater? Leave the area right away and do not touch switches, the heater or anything that could spark. From a safe distance, call 911 and Southwest Gas at 877-860-6020. Keep children away from any pool without a working barrier or cover, and never leave a child alone near water.
