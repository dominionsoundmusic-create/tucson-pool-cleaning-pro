What the white line on pool tile actually is

Pool water carries dissolved minerals, mainly calcium and magnesium. When the sun pulls water off the surface, only the water leaves. The minerals stay. Along the waterline the tile is wet one moment and drying the next, so a thin film keeps evaporating there and leaving a little more mineral behind each time. A pool trade article on waterline scale calls this deposit an evaporite and explains that balancing the water controls scale below the surface but cannot fully stop it at the tile line, because the film simply dries and the minerals cannot go with it.

Two kinds of scale show up on tile. Calcium carbonate is the common white, chalky crust. Calcium silicate forms more slowly, looks grayer and, according to pool maintenance writers, is much harder to get off. A quick acid test is one way trade writers tell them apart: carbonate fizzes and silicate barely reacts. That test belongs to the pool professional, and it is one reason an old, gray ring may need a different method than a fresh white one.

Why Tucson tile scales so quickly

Two local facts work together. First, the water going into the pool is hard. Tucson Water delivers Colorado River water brought in by the Central Arizona Project (CAP), recharged underground in Avra Valley and blended with local groundwater. The utility says the groundwater is naturally hard and that hardness rises as the share of CAP water rises. It also says hard water is safe to drink. Tucson Water does not publish one average hardness figure for the city, but its online water quality map shows readings near your address. North of the city, Oro Valley Water Utility reports hardness from 36 to 270 parts per million across its system.

Second, a lot of that water leaves by evaporation. By the Arizona Department of Water Resources' estimate, an open water surface in Tucson loses roughly six feet a year, mostly during the summer months. Every inch you top off brings in a fresh load of minerals, and every inch that evaporates leaves them in the pool. Over a few summers the pool concentrates calcium, and the tile line is where you see it first.

  • Hard fill waterCalcium arrives with every top off from the hose or autofill.
  • High evaporationAbout six feet a year leaves the minerals behind (ADWR).
  • Wet and dry cyclingSplash and small level changes keep the tile band drying over and over.
  • Unbalanced waterWater that leans toward scaling drops calcium faster on every surface.

Pool tile cleaning methods in Tucson, compared

A careful pool technician starts by looking at the scale and the tile together. How thick is the crust? Is it white and flaky or gray and glassy? Is the tile glossy glazed ceramic, glass mosaic or an older, rougher tile, and is the grout sound? The answers point to a method. Gentle hand methods

Brushing the tile band during routine service costs little and slows a new line, and it is part of what a good weekly visit should include (see pool cleaning and weekly maintenance). Brushing will not remove scale that has hardened into a crust. Pumice is a soft volcanic stone sold for pool scale, and it works on light deposits, but it is still an abrasive. On glass tile or high gloss glaze, a scratch is permanent, so ask before you rub.

Chemical descalers

Descaling products dissolve carbonate scale. Most of them are acids, which is the reason this page gives no mixing or application steps. Acid can etch grout and cement finishes, it reacts dangerously with other pool chemicals, and the CDC warns never to mix pool chemicals with each other. A professional who uses a descaler should be able to tell you what it is, how the tile and the water will be protected, and how the pool's chemistry will be corrected afterward.

Media blasting

For thick scale, many companies use a blaster that aims fine media at the scale band, often with the water lowered below the tile. A pool trade article describes bead blasting as a gentler form of sandblasting and says whether it suits a pool depends on the tile: solid porcelain usually tolerates it, while rougher, slate-look tiles call for real care and a small test patch first. Media and pressure choices vary from company to company, so the useful question is not "do you blast?" but "which media, at what pressure, and is it safe on my tile?" One pool industry patent filing also notes that fine blasting media can plug a pool's filtering system, so ask how spent media is cleaned up.

If the job needs it, the water is lowered below the tile band. The scale is removed section by section, the tile is rinsed and inspected, and the pool is refilled. If the water has to come down, it has to go somewhere. In Tucson and unincorporated Pima County the preferred route is the home's own sewer cleanout, and water may only go on the ground if it soaks in on your lot. Our guide to draining a pool in Tucson covers those rules.

A few loose tiles or cracked grout?

Cleaning sometimes shows problems the scale was hiding: hollow or loose tiles, missing grout or a crack behind the tile band. Those are repairs, not cleaning, and they can let water into the bond beam behind the tile. Ask the company to point out anything it finds, and see swimming pool repair in Tucson for what repair work involves.

Licensing. The ROC does not say plainly whether cleaning tile alone needs a contractor license, while fixing loose tile or grout is pool repair. The Arizona Registrar of Contractors (ROC) issues the R-6 license, Swimming Pool Service and Repair, for servicing and minor repairs on home pools; building pools and full interior replacement fall under licenses such as B-5. Ask any company for its ROC number, then enter it in the ROC's online contractor search to see whether the license is current, what class it is and whether complaints are on file. Apart from workers' compensation, the ROC does not require its licensees to carry insurance, so ask to see proof of liability coverage yourself.

Keeping the line from coming back

Clean tile starts collecting minerals again the day the pool is refilled. You cannot stop evaporation, but you can slow the buildup.

Helps

  • Keep the water balanced. Pool chemists use the Langelier Saturation Index (LSI), a number that shows whether water tends to dissolve or deposit calcium. The Pool and Hot Tub Alliance (PHTA) cites a range of minus 0.3 to plus 0.5 for proper balance.
  • Test the water with a home test kit and let your pool service adjust it.
  • Brush the tile band during routine cleaning.
  • Cover the pool between swims. According to ADWR, covering a pool stops 90 to 95 percent of its evaporation loss, so far less hard water gets added back.
  • Ask about a tile sealer or barrier product. Trade writers describe silicone based coatings that make it harder for calcium to bond to glaze.

Hurts

  • Letting the line go for years until it turns gray and hard.
  • Scraping with metal tools or wire brushes, which scratch the glaze.
  • Mixing pool chemicals or household cleaners. The CDC says never mix them.
  • Running the water with high pH and alkalinity for weeks.
  • Topping off again and again for years without ever diluting the water.
