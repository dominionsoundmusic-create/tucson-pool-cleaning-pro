Why a pool light is electrical work for a professional

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) notes that the National Electrical Code has required ground fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) protection for underwater pool lighting since 1968. A GFCI watches the current on a circuit and cuts the power in a fraction of a second when some of it leaks away, for example into water. The CPSC and the American Red Cross warn that older pools are the biggest concern, because underwater lights may have worn over the years and may not be GFCI protected at all.

The National Fire Protection Association calls the danger electric shock drowning: current in the water can disable a swimmer's muscles so they cannot stay afloat. You cannot see it or smell it.

Stop and keep everyone out of the water if a light is cracked, fogged or loose, if its GFCI or breaker trips again after one reset, or if anyone feels a tingle. Leave the light switched off. Do not open the fixture, pull it from the wall, change the bulb or open any electrical box. Call a qualified professional.

The monthly GFCI test

This is the one electrical check an owner should do, and the CPSC says to do it at least monthly. Find the GFCI that protects the pool light. It may be a breaker with a test button in the panel or a subpanel, or a GFCI outlet near the equipment pad; a pool professional can show you which one if you are not sure.

  1. Turn the pool light on and keep everyone out of the water.
  2. Press the GFCI's test button. The light should go out right away.
  3. Press reset (or switch the GFCI breaker back on). The light should come back.
  4. If it fails either step, meaning the light stays on when tested or the GFCI will not reset, leave the light off and have it checked before anyone swims at night.

A GFCI that trips by itself is doing its job. It is telling you something on the circuit is leaking current. Resetting it over and over is not the fix.

What a pool light repair involves

A careful technician starts by shutting the power off at the breaker and confirming it is dead before touching the fixture. On many pools the light is held in its niche by a single screw, and the cord is left long enough that the fixture can be lifted out onto the deck while the pool stays full. From there, the repair is usually one of these:

  • Lens gasket or sealThe rubber ring that keeps water out of the fixture hardens with age and heat. A new gasket, and sometimes a new lens, stops the leak if the inside has not been damaged.
  • Bulb or LED moduleA failed bulb is replaced inside the sealed fixture, then the fixture is resealed and tested.
  • LED conversionAn old incandescent fixture is swapped for an LED fixture that fits the existing niche.
  • Cord and nicheA cord that is cracked or has wicked water, or a damaged conduit, may mean pulling a new cord or replacing the fixture.
  • Low-voltage systemsLights run through a listed transformer add the transformer itself as something to test.

After any repair, the technician should test the GFCI, check the bonding and run the light long enough to be sure the seal holds. Light niches are also a common place for a pool to leak water, so if the level drops and stops at the light, read pool leak and line repair.

Who is allowed to do the work

Fine for the owner

  • Switching the light on and off
  • Checking the light's timer or automation schedule
  • Testing the GFCI monthly and resetting a trip once
  • Looking at the light from the deck for fog, cracks or a loose fixture
  • Keeping swimmers out until it is checked

Leave to a qualified professional

  • Removing the fixture from the niche
  • Opening the lens or changing the bulb
  • Anything inside a junction box, transformer, panel or breaker
  • New cords, new fixtures and LED conversions
  • Testing bonding and grounding

In Arizona, the Registrar of Contractors' R-6 license for pool service and repair leaves out electrical work beyond the first disconnect. The ROC's residential electrical classification is R-11. So before a light repair, ask the company which parts its own license covers and whether an electrical contractor will handle the wiring.

Pool lights and Tucson summers

Night swimming is part of a Tucson summer, because evenings stay warm: National Weather Service normals put the average July low at Tucson International Airport at about 76 degrees. That means the light gets more use in the same months its gasket is baking in pool water warmed by afternoon highs near 100 degrees. It is a good reason to test the GFCI each month through the summer and to look at the lens whenever the pool is clear and calm.

Monsoon season, set by the National Weather Service in Tucson as June 15 through September 30, carries lightning as well as rain and dust. Get out of the pool when you hear thunder, whatever the state of the light, and wait until the storm has passed. After a storm knocks the power out, check the light's timer or automation clock, since an outage can reset it.
