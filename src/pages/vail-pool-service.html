Vail: a growing community southeast of Tucson

Vail is not a city or town. It is an unincorporated community in Pima County, counted by the Census Bureau as a census designated place. The 2020 Census found 13,604 people there, up from 10,208 in 2010. Recent Census survey figures describe a place of family households: about 2.95 people per household, nearly a quarter of residents under 18, and an owner occupied rate of about 91%.

The setting is open desert below the Rincon Mountains. Pima County's Colossal Cave Mountain Park sits in the Rincon foothills nearby, and the county's Cienega Creek Natural Preserve, about 25 miles southeast of downtown Tucson, protects stretches of creek that run year round. Large planned communities are part of the picture too: Pima County's list of specific plans includes Vail Valley Ranch, known as Rancho del Lago, and Rocking K, and the county's area policy calls for trails linking the two.

Pool barriers in Vail follow Pima County's rules

With so many children in Vail homes, the fence and gate around the pool matter as much as the water in it. Because Vail has no town government, the county's residential barrier rules are the ones that apply.

Height and gaps

Measured from the ground on the outside, the top of the barrier must reach 48 inches or more under Pima County's rules, and the bottom must sit no more than 2 inches above grade. Gaps must be too small for a 4-inch sphere, and chain link or lattice openings are limited to 1.75 inches.

Gates

Pedestrian gates must open outward, away from the pool, and must close and latch on their own. A gate propped open for a party or a delivery defeats the whole barrier, so check that it swings shut and catches every time.

Every day habits

A child should never be near the water without an adult watching, even for a moment, and keep children away from any pool that is unfenced or uncovered. A green or cloudy pool is a hazard in itself, because nobody can see the bottom.

Water, power and sewer: what Vail homes have

Vail Water and the CAP

Vail Water Company is a private water company, separate from Tucson Water. Its Colorado River allocation from the Central Arizona Project is sent to Tucson Water's recharge facilities, stored underground, recovered and moved through Tucson's distribution system to Vail by way of the Old Vail Reservoir. Tucson Water calls this wheeling. Vail Water's annual report for the year ending December 2025 describes its supply as 100% CAP water. Some addresses in the wider area are served by other providers, including the small Rincon Water Company, so your bill is the place to confirm yours.

We did not find a published hardness number for Vail Water, and we will not invent one. What is certain is that a pool concentrates minerals as it loses water to the sun. By the Arizona Department of Water Resources' estimate, roughly six feet of water a year evaporates from an open surface here, and every refill brings more calcium in. A careful pool technician adds a calcium hardness test to the usual chlorine and pH readings and can tell you when scale is starting on the tile.

Power from TEP or Trico

Electric service in Vail depends on the address. Tucson Electric Power has a substation in Vail, and Trico Electric Cooperative has a board district for Vail and Corona de Tucson. Your bill tells you which one you have. If you are on a TEP time of use plan, TEP's summer on-peak window runs from 3 p.m. to 7 p.m., Monday to Friday, and the utility recommends a morning or midday pump schedule; check your own plan's current hours.

Sewer or septic before you drain

When a pool needs fresh water, the City of Tucson and Pima County guidelines prefer the sanitary sewer: a hose and a small pump into your own property's cleanout, at low-use hours, after the water has gone at least 72 hours without chlorine and its pH sits between 7 and 8. Filter backwash may stay on your lot only if the ground absorbs it within a day and none of it runs to a neighbor's lot, the street or the alley. Not every Vail home is on sewer, though. If yours has a septic system, check with Pima County's onsite wastewater program before draining, and the draining guide covers the rest of the rules.

Monsoon storms off the mountains

The National Weather Service in Tucson sets the monsoon season from June 15 to September 30. Gusts racing out ahead of a storm can carry dust, leaves and twigs into a yard in minutes, and a heavy rain dilutes chlorine just when the water is warm. Once the storm passes, look at the baskets, listen for the pump, and run a water test before the kids go back in.

  • Baskets. Wind and rain fill the skimmer and pump baskets fast, so clear both.
  • Pressure gauge. Note the filter pressure reading so you can tell the pool company what you see.
  • Timer. Storm outages can wipe a pump schedule; confirm the clock still shows the right time.
  • Breaker. One reset of a tripped breaker or GFCI is fine. A second trip is a sign of an electrical fault, so stop there and phone for help.
  • Storage. Keep every pool product in its original container and never combine two of them. Store them apart, dry, shaded and under lock, well away from children.

Smell gas near a pool heater? Leave the area right away and do not touch switches, the heater or anything that could make a spark. Once you are a safe distance away, call 911 and Southwest Gas at 877-860-6020.
