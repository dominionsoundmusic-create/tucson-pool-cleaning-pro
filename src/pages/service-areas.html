Searching for pool service in Arizona usually turns up companies all over the state. This line is narrower on purpose: it covers the Tucson area and nothing else, so the person you end up talking with works in Pima County and knows its water, its storms and its rules.

How coverage works

There is no office or service yard behind this line, in Tucson or anywhere else. It is one phone number. An automated assistant answers it day or night, learns the problem and roughly where you live, and connects you with a pool company, locally owned and independent of the line, that covers that part of the metro. The company then quotes and handles the job on its own terms, and you pay it directly.

Because the companies are independent, the line does not promise that a particular company will be free on a particular day, and it does not set prices anywhere. What stays the same in every community is the call itself: free, answered 24 hours a day, and passed to a local business rather than a national call center.

Tucson

Most calls come from the city itself, from midtown and the university neighborhoods to the east side toward the Rincon Mountains, the west side below the Tucson Mountains, and the southeast. Inside city limits, Tucson Water supplies most homes with river water brought by the Central Arizona Project canal and recharged underground in Avra Valley and blended with groundwater. The city says hardness rises as the CAP share rises, and you can look up the readings near your address on Tucson Water's water quality map.

Most city homes get their power from Tucson Electric Power (TEP), though your bill is the final word, and City of Tucson guidance governs where drained pool water and filter backwash may go: the private sewer cleanout first, with backwash kept on your own lot. Unincorporated neighborhoods just outside the city, such as Casas Adobes, follow Pima County instead and may have a different water provider, including Metro Water District on parts of the northwest side.

Communities with their own page

Each town page goes deeper into local water, power and pool rules. Here is the short version.

Pool service in Oro Valley

An incorporated town north of Tucson with its own water utility. Oro Valley Water Utility reported that its 2023 drinking water was about two thirds groundwater and one third CAP water, with hardness from 36 to 270 parts per million depending on the area. Ask the town about any local rules for draining a pool.

Pool service in the Catalina Foothills

An unincorporated community, counted at about 52,400 people in the 2020 Census, on the slopes below the Santa Catalina Mountains, so Pima County is the local government. Much of it is served by Tucson Water; your bill or the utility's service area map confirms your provider.

Pool service in Vail

An unincorporated community southeast of Tucson. One of its providers, Vail Water Company, is a private utility separate from Tucson Water; its CAP water is stored and recovered by Tucson Water and sent through the city's pipes to reach Vail. Electric service comes from TEP or Trico Electric Cooperative depending on the address.

Pool service in Sahuarita

An incorporated town south of Tucson along Interstate 19. The town itself does not sell water; it lists seven water providers in the Sahuarita area. Power comes from TEP or Trico depending on where you live.

Pool service in Green Valley

An unincorporated community farther south on Interstate 19, under Pima County. The largest water provider is Community Water Company of Green Valley, which relies on groundwater; the Green Valley Domestic Water Improvement District and Farmers Water Co. serve other parts.

Marana

Marana has no page of its own because searches for pool care there are rare, but its calls are taken the same way. The Town of Marana runs its own water utility, Marana Water, and Trico Electric Cooperative is headquartered there, though some addresses are TEP customers.

What changes from one community to the next

The pool itself does not care which side of a town line it sits on, but a few practical things do change.

  • Fill waterEach provider blends groundwater and CAP water differently, so hardness varies. Harder water means faster scale on tile and in heaters and salt cells. Your provider's annual water quality report or its customer line can tell you what comes out of your hose.
  • Electric providerTEP serves the city and much of the metro, while Trico serves many surrounding communities. If your plan has time-of-use pricing, the hours your pump runs affect the bill. TEP's summer on-peak hours are weekday afternoons from 3 to 7 p.m.; check your own plan.
  • Draining and backwash rulesThe City of Tucson and Pima County share the same basic guidance: drain through your property's sewer cleanout, wait at least 72 hours after the last chlorine, and keep backwash on your own land. Incorporated towns may add their own requirements.
  • Pool barriersPima County sets barrier rules for pools in unincorporated areas, and the city and towns apply their own building codes. A fence company or your local building office can confirm what applies to you.

The same weather everywhere

Whatever the address, every pool in the area lives through the same summers. Monsoon season, by the National Weather Service calendar, starts June 15 and ends September 30, when dust storms and downpours load filters and use up chlorine. The heat before the storms is just as hard on the water, which is why so many owners keep a weekly schedule all summer. The Tucson pool maintenance schedule shows what to watch each season.
