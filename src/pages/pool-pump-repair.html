The pump is the heart of the pool: it pulls water from the skimmer and main drain, pushes it through the filter and sends it back through the return jets. When it stops, or keeps running but moves less water, the chlorine stops spreading and the filter stops catching dust. On a 100 degree afternoon that shows up in the water fast.

What is inside a pool pump, and what wears out

Most home pool pumps share the same basic layout. Knowing the names of the parts makes it easier to follow what a technician tells you and to compare two quotes.

Strainer pot and lid

The clear-lidded chamber at the front holds a basket that catches leaves and hair before they reach the impeller. The lid seals with an O-ring. A cracked lid or a dry, flattened O-ring lets air in, and air is the usual reason a pump loses prime.

Impeller

The spinning part that actually moves water. Debris that gets past the basket can jam or chip it, and a worn or clogged impeller means weak flow even though the motor sounds normal.

Shaft seal

A two-part seal keeps water in the pump from running down the motor shaft. When it wears, you see drips under the pump or water near the motor. Angi suggests seals are often replaced every two to three years, or sooner if they leak.

Motor and bearings

The electric motor turns the impeller. Its bearings can wear and start to screech or grind. A motor that overheats may shut itself off and restart once it cools, which is a sign something is making it work too hard.

Capacitor and controls

Many single-speed motors use a capacitor to start. When it fails, the motor may hum without turning. Variable-speed pumps add an electronic drive and keypad. Capacitors can hold a charge after the power is off, so this is a job for a professional.

Housing and unions

The pump body and the threaded unions that join it to the plumbing. Years of sun and heat can make plastic brittle, and a crack in the housing usually tips the decision toward a new pump.

Hard water reaches the pump too

Tucson Water's own hard water page says local groundwater is naturally hard, meaning it carries dissolved calcium and magnesium, and that hardness goes up as more Central Arizona Project (CAP) water is blended in. Evaporation takes the water but not the minerals, which can leave white scale on fittings, seals and the inside of the pump. Scale on a seal face or a union can turn into a slow drip that gets worse over a summer.

When a technician opens a pump and finds scale, it is worth asking about the water balance too. Fixing the seal without asking why it scaled up can mean the same repair again later. The weekly cleaning and maintenance page covers how routine testing helps.

Fix the pump or replace it: four questions

Can a pool pump be repaired? Often, but the honest answer depends on what failed, how old the pump is and what the alternative costs. Working through these questions with the technician keeps the decision clear.

  1. What exactly failed? A leaking seal, a clogged impeller or a bad capacitor is a parts job. A cracked housing or a burned-out motor on an old pump is a bigger conversation. Ask the technician to name the failed part and show it to you if they can.
  2. How old is the pump? Angi puts a typical pool pump's life at roughly eight to 15 years, depending on size, type and upkeep. A repair on a two-year-old pump is an easy call. On a pump in its teens, money spent on one part may be followed by another failure.
  3. How does the repair compare with a new pump? A common rule of thumb in the HomeAdvisor and Angi pump guides is to lean toward replacement when the repair would cost more than about half the price of a new pump. Ask for both prices so you can compare.
  4. What will a new motor have to be? If the motor needs replacing, federal rules shape what can be sold (see below). A variable-speed motor costs more up front and can cut the power bill. That trade-off belongs in the decision.

Pool pump replacement in Tucson and the federal motor rule

The U.S. Department of Energy set efficiency standards for the motors that drive pool pumps. Most motors rated between 1.15 and 5 total horsepower that left the factory on September 29, 2025 or later are required to offer variable-speed control. Smaller motors, from 0.5 to under 1.15 horsepower, have a later date of September 28, 2027, and an April 2026 DOE enforcement policy delays enforcement for those small motors made before March 26, 2029.

What this means for a Tucson homeowner:

  • Nobody has to replace a pump that still works. The rule applies to motors as they are manufactured.
  • If your single-speed motor fails, the replacement offered is likely to be variable speed, which affects the quote.
  • ENERGY STAR notes that cutting a pump's speed in half lets it use about one-eighth as much energy, which is why slower, longer runs can save power.
  • Ask the company how it will set the speeds and run times for your pool once the new motor is in.

Running a pool pump on Tucson power and in Tucson heat

A pump in Tucson works through long, hot summers, and how it runs affects both its life and your bill.

  • Your power companyDepending on your address, your electricity comes from Tucson Electric Power (TEP) or Trico Electric Cooperative. Your bill tells you which.
  • TEP on-peak hoursOn TEP's time-of-use plans, summer on-peak runs 3 to 7 p.m. on weekdays. TEP suggests programming the pool pump to run in the morning or at midday. Check your plan for its current hours.
  • Heat on the equipment padThe National Weather Service counts an average of 68 days a year at or above 100 degrees in Tucson (1991 to 2020 normals). A motor that is already working hard, with a clogged basket or a blocked vent, can overheat on those afternoons and shut itself off.
  • Dust and debrisMonsoon season, June 15 to September 30, brings storms whose outflow winds can push dust and leaves into the pool. That load ends up in the skimmer and pump baskets, so they fill faster in summer.

Keeping the area around the motor clear of leaves, gravel and stored items helps it shed heat. For what to do through the seasons, see the Tucson pool maintenance schedule.

How a pump repair visit usually goes

A good pump visit starts with listening and looking, not with parts. A technician will usually check that the pump has power and is primed, listen to the motor for humming, grinding or screeching, look for drips at the seal and around the unions, check the filter pressure and look at the baskets and impeller for debris. On a pump that keeps shutting off, they look at what is making the motor overheat. Electrical testing, such as checking a capacitor, is done with the right tools and with the power handled safely.

Before you agree to anything, it helps to ask:

  • Which part failed, and how do you know?
  • What would the repair cost, and what would a replacement pump cost, installed?
  • If you replace the motor, will the new one be variable speed, and how will you set it?
  • Does the work need an electrician, or a permit, and who arranges that?
  • Which Arizona ROC license do you hold, and what is the number?
