Tucson pool water recycling and draining both answer the same problem: after a few seasons in the desert, the water in a pool can hold so many dissolved minerals that balancing it gets harder and scale keeps coming back. Fresh water is the cure. This guide explains why the water wears out, how to tell it is time, how a partial drain, a full drain and on-site recycling compare, and exactly where City of Tucson and Pima County rules let the old water go.

Why pool water gets "old" in Tucson

Water leaves a pool in two ways. Some of it goes out through splash-out and filter backwashing, and that water carries its minerals with it. Most of it, in the desert, simply evaporates. Evaporation only removes pure water. The calcium, magnesium, salts and leftover chemical byproducts stay behind, and the water that remains gets a little more concentrated every day.

Then you top off. Tucson Water's supply is hard, the utility explains, because local groundwater is naturally hard and hardness climbs as the share of Central Arizona Project (CAP) water in the blend rises. Every refill brings in a fresh load of minerals. A pool trade article on hot, sunny climates sums the cycle up well: evaporating and refilling with hard water is almost designed to concentrate calcium and other dissolved solids. According to Arizona Department of Water Resources figures, an open water surface in Tucson loses about six feet a year, and says a typical pool's yearly evaporation, refills and backwashing add up to roughly a whole pool's worth of water.

Two terms come up when a pool company talks about old water. Total dissolved solids (TDS) means the sum of all the material carried in solution: minerals, salt and the residue of years of chemicals. Calcium hardness is the calcium part of that. A third, cyanuric acid, is the stabilizer that shields chlorine from sunlight. It is a useful tool under the Tucson sun, but it builds up when stabilized chlorine products are used, and the CDC notes that chlorine works more slowly when cyanuric acid is present. None of the three leaves the pool by evaporation. They only go down when water is replaced or filtered out.

Signs the water needs replacing

A water test, not the calendar, tells you when. A pool technician reads these results for clues.

  • High total dissolved solidsThe Pool and Hot Tub Alliance (PHTA) guidance is that TDS should rise no more than 1,500 ppm above the level the pool started with; beyond that, partial or full draining may be needed.
  • Calcium that keeps climbingScale returns quickly on the tile and equipment even after the water is balanced.
  • Too much stabilizerCyanuric acid readings are so elevated that the chlorine in the pool works sluggishly.
  • Hard to balance waterChemistry drifts and readings will not hold even with regular service.
  • Work that needs an empty shellReplastering, major tile work or some repairs cannot be done with water in the pool.

Partial drain, full drain or Tucson pool water recycling

There are three ways to get fresh water into a pool, and they trade water use, cost and risk against each other.

Partial drain and refill

Part of the water is pumped out and replaced. Replacing a third of the water dilutes what is dissolved in it by roughly a third, less whatever the new hard water brings in. The shell stays mostly covered, so the risks below are lower. It may not be enough when levels are very high.

Full drain and refill

All of the water goes and the pool starts over with fresh fill water. It is the only choice when the shell has to be empty for replastering or repairs. It uses the most water and leaves the finish and shell exposed, so timing and planning matter most here.

On-site water recycling

A mobile reverse osmosis unit filters the pool water and returns it, lowering TDS, calcium, salt and cyanuric acid without emptying the pool. Some concentrated waste water is still produced and must be disposed of, but it is a fraction of a full drain.

How mobile pool water recycling works

Reverse osmosis (RO) pushes water through membranes fine enough to hold back dissolved minerals and salts. For pools, the equipment is usually built onto a truck. Hoses run from the pool to the unit, the water is pumped through the membranes and the filtered water goes straight back into the pool, which usually stays full while the job runs. Trade coverage describes it as a way to lower TDS, calcium, salt and stabilizer without a drain. The leftover concentrate, which holds the minerals that were removed, still has to go somewhere, and the same city and county discharge rules apply to it. One residential case study in AQUA Magazine reported about 19,500 gallons of water kept compared with draining and refilling that pool.

Not every pool company owns this equipment. When the line connects you, find out whether that company offers recycling or can arrange it, how much water it expects to recover for your pool, and where the concentrate will be discharged. Recycling also skips one problem with draining: the refill water in Tucson is hard to begin with, so a fresh fill restarts the mineral cycle from a fairly high base.

Tucson and Pima County rules on draining pool water

The City of Tucson and Pima County's Regional Wastewater Reclamation Department publish shared guidelines for pool and spa water. In plain terms, they say:

Allowed and preferred

  • Discharge to the sanitary sewer through the access cap of your own property's sewer cleanout, using a hose and a small pump.
  • Pump during low-use sewer hours and control the flow so the cleanout never overflows.
  • Leave the water untreated for at least 72 hours after the last chlorine, then measure the pH and adjust it to 7 to 8.
  • Filter backwash may go on your own land only if it soaks into the ground within 24 hours and never reaches a neighbor's property, the street or an alley.

Not allowed

  • Using public manholes or public cleanouts.
  • Running water down the curb, into storm drains or into washes when a private cleanout exists. That route is a last resort only, and only for clear, dechlorinated water in the 7 to 8 pH range that causes no erosion and stays off neighbors' property and sidewalks.
  • Letting acid wash water run off into the curb, storm drains or washes.
  • Letting backwash flow off your property.
  • Connecting a pool permanently to a storm drain or wash.

The city notes that backwash water often carries chlorine, germs and other contaminants that should stay on site, and its inspectors do respond to reports of pool water in the street. If you have questions, the city's Stormwater Management Section is at (520) 791-4251, and Pima County's wastewater department is at (520) 724-6500. If you live in Oro Valley, Marana, Sahuarita or another town, also check that town's rules before anyone pumps.

The risks of an empty pool in the Tucson heat

The finish dries out

The National Plasterers Council explains that cement based pool finishes are meant to stay underwater. Exposure to air starts drying them, hot, dry or windy weather speeds that up, and long exposure can damage the finish. The council ranks weather as the leading factor in when and how long a pool can stay empty, and says rescheduling is the best choice when extreme weather is expected. Tucson's normal July high is about 100 degrees, so a midsummer drain asks a lot of the plaster.

The shell can be pushed up

A full pool is held down by the weight of its water. Empty, it can be lifted by water pressure in the ground around it. Pool trade sources describe shells pushed up and tilted out of the ground, and fiberglass shells that buckle, and say vinyl liners can float. Their advice before draining is to check the water table and make sure the pool's hydrostatic relief valve works. This is a key reason the decision belongs with a pool professional.

Who to call and what to ask

A pool service company is the right call for any drain or recycling job. It can test the water, judge the shell and finish, handle the pump and hoses, keep the discharge legal and restart the pool. Leave acid washing and any chemical work to the professional. The CDC is clear that pool chemicals must never be mixed with each other, and acid wash water has its own discharge limits.
