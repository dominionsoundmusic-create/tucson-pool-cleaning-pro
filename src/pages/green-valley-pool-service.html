Green Valley: a retirement community on I-19

Green Valley is an unincorporated community in Pima County, strung along Interstate 19 south of Sahuarita. The Census Bureau counted 22,616 residents in 2020, up from 21,391 in 2010. Its age profile is unusual: about 80.5% of residents are 65 or older, and about 86% of homes are owner occupied.

Green Valley Recreation (GVR) runs pools and spas at its recreation centers for its member households. Those are shared facilities, and this page is not about them. It is for the homes that also keep a private pool, where the owner is responsible for the water, the equipment and the gate. To the east stand the Santa Rita Mountains. From Exit 63, Continental Road leads toward Madera Canyon on the range's north side, and the Forest Service says Mount Wrightson rises more than 7,000 feet above the surrounding lowlands.

Who provides water for Green Valley, AZ

There is no single Green Valley water department. Several separate systems serve the community, all pumping local groundwater. The name on your bill tells you which one fills your pool.

Community Water Company of Green Valley

The main provider. It has run the system since June 1977, a federal water system record lists about 22,085 people served, and the company has pursued Central Arizona Project water to take pressure off the aquifer.

Green Valley Domestic Water Improvement District

A public district that residents formed in May 2003. Its area lies south of Mission Twin Buttes, mostly on the west side of I-19, with about 4,400 residential connections.

Farmers Water Co

A groundwater system listed in Green Valley, serving about 4,290 people through roughly 1,700 connections, according to the federal public water system record.

Valle Verde del Norte Water Cooperative

A small water cooperative named for, and serving, the Valle Verde del Norte community. The Town of Sahuarita lists it among the area's providers.

What groundwater and heat mean for a Green Valley pool

No published hardness number for any Green Valley provider turned up in our research, so we leave it off. Each provider's annual water quality report may carry the figure, and a home test kit or a test by the pool company gives you the number for your own pool. That second number matters more, because a pool keeps every mineral it is filled with while the desert sun pulls water out. Open water around Tucson loses close to six feet a year to evaporation, by the reckoning of the state's Department of Water Resources. Calcium hardness, meaning the dissolved calcium the refills leave behind, keeps rising until scale forms on tile and inside equipment.

That is why regular pool service in a desert yard includes more than skimming. A careful pool technician keeps chlorine and pH where the CDC suggests for home pools (pH 7.0 to 7.8, free chlorine of at least 1 ppm, or at least 2 ppm when stabilizer is used) and keeps an eye on calcium so it can recommend partial draining before scale takes hold. Draining to the county sewer

Pima County's Regional Wastewater Reclamation Department runs a treatment plant in Green Valley, and the shared City of Tucson and Pima County guidelines treat the sanitary sewer as the preferred home for old pool water. The water should sit untreated for at least 72 hours after the last chlorine, have its pH brought to 7 to 8, and go slowly through the cleanout on your own lot using a hose and a small pump during low-use hours. Backwash may soak into your own yard if it is absorbed within 24 hours and stays off the street and the neighbors' property. Water from an acid wash never qualifies for the street. The guide to draining a pool covers the details.
