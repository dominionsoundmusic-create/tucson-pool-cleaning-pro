Weekly pool service in Tucson is less about one cleaning and more about an ongoing arrangement: a company visits on a set day, keeps the water safe and clear, watches the equipment, and bills you on a schedule you agreed to. Getting that arrangement right at the start, in writing, saves arguments later. This page explains what a typical plan covers, how often a Tucson pool needs a visit, how salt water pools fit in, and what to settle before you hand someone your gate code.

Weekly or every other week: choosing a visit schedule

A pool's needs change with the season, and Tucson's seasons are sharp. Weather Service climate normals for 1991 through 2020 put the average July high at Tucson International Airport at about 100 degrees, and on average about 68 days a year climb to 100 or higher. Hot water under a hard sun burns through chlorine fast, and algae grow fastest exactly when the pool gets the most use. That is why most Tucson plans are built around a weekly visit.

A weekly visit makes sense when

  • It is late spring, summer or monsoon season.
  • Trees, palms or open desert sit near the water.
  • Children, guests or dogs use the pool often.
  • You do not want to test the water yourself between visits.
  • The pool has a history of turning cloudy or green.

Every other week may be enough when

  • It is the cooler part of the year and the pool sees light use.
  • The yard is mostly gravel and block wall, with little leaf drop.
  • You are willing to empty baskets and run a home test kit in between.
  • The company agrees the pool holds its chemistry for two weeks.

Some companies switch customers between schedules by season; others offer one schedule only. Ask which approach the company takes before you compare prices, because a weekly quote and an every other week quote are not the same product.

Pool service for salt water pools

A salt pool is still a chlorine pool. The difference is where the chlorine comes from, and that changes a few items on the service list.

A salt chlorine generator passes an electric current through lightly salted water inside a cell on the return line, and that turns some of the salt into chlorine. The CDC describes the chlorine made this way as the same chlorine you would get from a jug or tablet, so the targets stay the same: pH between 7.0 and 7.8, and free chlorine of at least 1 part per million, or at least 2 when the water holds stabilizer.

What a salt pool adds is the cell. Tucson Water notes that local groundwater is naturally hard and that hardness rises as a larger share of Central Arizona Project (CAP) water, which comes from the Colorado River, goes into the blend. Hard water tends to leave white scale on the plates inside a salt cell, and a scaled cell makes less chlorine. Tucson Water's own advice is to maintain pool equipment according to the manufacturer's guidelines, and the cell maker's manual is where the inspection and cleaning schedule comes from.

What a written service agreement should state

A handshake plan is easy to start and hard to sort out when something goes wrong. The Arizona Registrar of Contractors (ROC) tells homeowners to get written estimates, at least three of them for contracted work, to confirm a license covers the work described, and to make sure the person they deal with really represents the licensed company. For a recurring plan, put these points on paper:

  • The visit day and frequency, and what happens when that day falls on a holiday.
  • The task list for a routine visit, matching the table above.
  • Chemicals: which are in the plan price, which are billed as used, and how each visit's additions get reported to you.
  • Extras: filter cleaning, salt cell cleaning, storm visits and tile work, each with its price or how it will be quoted.
  • Repairs: a written quote before any repair, and a dollar limit above which the company must ask first.
  • Billing: per visit or per month, the due date, and how a missed visit is credited.
  • Access: gate code or key, dogs, and who to call if the gate is locked.
  • Ending the plan: the notice period and how notice must be given.
  • The company's ROC license number, if it does repair work, and the proof of liability insurance you asked for.

License numbers belong in the agreement. The Arizona Registrar of Contractors issues an R-6 license, Residential Swimming Pool Service and Repair, for servicing home pools and making minor repairs; building a pool or replacing a full plaster or pebble interior falls under pool construction licenses such as B-5. The ROC does not say outright whether routine cleaning alone needs a license, so ask each company what it holds. Type the number into the ROC contractor search (new tab) to see the status, classification and complaints from the last two years. Tucson Pool Cleaning Pro does not run this check for you.

How to compare pool maintenance service companies

  1. Describe the same pool to each one. Size, spa or no spa, salt or chlorine, trees nearby, dogs, and anything already wrong. A quote is only as good as the description behind it.
  2. Get the task list in writing. Line the lists up and cross out what one company includes and another does not.
  3. Convert to a yearly figure. Per visit, per month and per season quotes only compare once they cover the same twelve months.
  4. Ask how they report. A note, an app or a door tag after each visit tells you what was tested and added.
  5. Check the license and insurance. The ROC search shows license status, classification and recent complaints, but not insurance, which the ROC does not require apart from workers' compensation. Ask for proof directly.
  6. Ask what happens when they see a problem. A good answer sounds like a photo, a written quote and your yes before anything is replaced.

Monsoon season, summer trips and your service plan

The National Weather Service defines Tucson's monsoon season as June 15 through September 30. Thunderstorm outflow can push a wall of dust ahead of it, and that dust, plus leaves and seed pods, ends up in the pool and the filter. Ask before the season starts whether the company comes back after a big storm or waits for the regular day, and what that costs.

Storm season also brings door knockers. The ROC has issued warnings about contracting scams during monsoon season, and the same habits apply to pool work: check the license, get the price in writing and do not pay large sums up front to someone you just met.

Summer trips are the other weak spot. Before you leave, tell the company your dates and give it an emergency contact. Make sure the pump timer is set and the water level is at the middle of the skimmer opening. The Arizona Department of Water Resources says roughly six feet of water a year evaporates from open water in Tucson, so a pool can drop noticeably in a couple of hot weeks. ADWR adds that a cover cuts that loss by 90 to 95 percent. A pool left untended in July can also go green, and Pima County asks residents to report green, neglected pools that breed mosquitoes at 520-724-7908. If yours has already turned, see green pool cleanup.

Ending or changing a pool service plan

People change plans when they sell a house, take over the work themselves or simply want a different company. A clean exit protects you and the next company:

  • Give notice the way the agreement asks, in writing, and keep a copy.
  • Ask for the most recent water test results and any notes on equipment problems.
  • Change the gate code or collect the key.
  • Check the final invoice against visits actually made.
  • If chemicals were left on site, keep them in their original labeled containers, dry, shaded and locked away from children and pets, as the CDC advises, and never combine one with another.

Between companies, the pool still needs care. Keep the pump running on its usual schedule, empty the baskets and test the water with a home kit until the new plan starts. TEP's time-of-use plans make weekday afternoons from 3 to 7 p.m. the summer peak, and TEP suggests running a pool pump in the morning or at midday instead; check your own plan's current hours.

Safe checks you can make between visits

Paying for a plan is no reason to ignore the pool all week. None of these checks takes tools or chemicals:

  • Water level at the skimmer, especially in June and after windy days.
  • Skimmer and pump baskets clear of leaves and seed pods.
  • The filter pressure gauge, compared with the reading the company tells you is normal.
  • The timer or automation schedule, after a power outage.
  • Drips or puddles at the equipment pad.
  • A tripped GFCI or breaker, reset once only. If it trips a second time, keep it off and have a professional find the cause.

Safety first. Never mix pool chemicals with each other, follow the label, and leave acid work and anything electrical or gas related to professionals. If you smell gas near a pool heater, leave the area right away, do not touch switches or the heater, and from a safe distance call 911 and Southwest Gas at 877-860-6020. Keep children away from an unfenced or uncovered pool at all times.
