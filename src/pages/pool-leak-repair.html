Pool leak repair in Tucson starts with a question many owners skip: is the water actually leaking, or is the desert simply drinking it? Tucson air is dry and summers are long, so a pool loses a noticeable amount of water to evaporation even when nothing is wrong. A real leak looks similar at first. This page shows you how to tell the two apart with a bucket, where leaks tend to hide in a pool, how professional leak detection works, what a repair can involve and what published guides say it costs.

Finding and fixing leaks is not something Tucson Pool Cleaning Pro does. What it offers is a free phone number: an automated assistant picks up at any hour, notes the problem and the part of town, and transfers the call to a pool company in the Tucson area that is independent and locally owned. That company looks for the leak, quotes the repair and charges you directly.

Why Tucson pools lose water even without a leak

The Arizona Department of Water Resources (ADWR) puts the average evaporation rate for Tucson at roughly six feet of water a year, with most of it in the summer months. Spread evenly, that is close to a fifth of an inch a day, but evenly is not how it happens: June and July, before and during the early monsoon, pull far more than a cool January day. The National Weather Service in Tucson counts about 68 days a year at 100 degrees or hotter, and dry wind on top of heat speeds the loss further.

Splashing, a spillover spa, a waterfall and backwashing all add to it. So a pool that drops a little every week in summer is normal. A pool that drops much faster than its neighbors', drops the same amount on a cool still day as on a windy hot one, or keeps losing water with a cover on deserves a closer look.

ADWR adds that covering the pool holds back 90 to 95 percent of what the sun and wind would take, which is worth knowing whether or not you have a leak. Monsoon storms can confuse things the other way: a heavy downpour raises the level and hides a slow leak for a week. Watch the level again once the weather settles.

The bucket test: a safe check before you call

This test compares your pool with a sample of the same water sitting in the same sun and wind. If both drop by the same amount, evaporation explains the loss. If the pool drops more, the difference is probably a leak. It takes no chemicals and no tools beyond a bucket and a marker or tape.

  1. Top up the pool to its normal level and let it settle. Skip the test right after rain or on a day with a storm in the forecast.
  2. Fill a bucket with pool water to a few inches from the top and stand it on one of the top steps, so it sits in the pool water at the same temperature. Weigh it down if needed.
  3. Mark the water level inside the bucket, and mark the pool level on the outside of the bucket or on the skimmer face with tape.
  4. Run the pump on its normal schedule, keep swimmers out and leave autofill turned off if you have one.
  5. Compare after 24 hours. If the pool level fell noticeably more than the bucket level, write down both drops. Some leak specialists then repeat the test with the pump off for a day, because a leak that grows when the pump runs points to the plumbing.

Where pool leaks usually hide

Leaks fall into two groups: the shell and its fittings, and the plumbing that carries water to and from the equipment.

Common pool leak locations, what you might notice and how a professional checks them

Look at the equipment pad first; it is the one place where you can often see a leak with your own eyes. If you find a drip there, a pool company can usually fix it without any detection work.

How pool leak detection works

Professional leak detection is a process of elimination. A careful technician starts with the pad and the visible fittings, then works into the pool and finally into the buried pipe. The usual tools are simple in principle:

  • Dye testingA small puff of colored dye released next to a suspected crack or fitting, with the water still. If the dye gets pulled into the gap, water is escaping there. It is a quick way to check one suspected spot, such as the skimmer seam.
  • Pressure testingEach plumbing line is plugged at the pool end and pressurized with air or water. A line that cannot hold pressure has a break somewhere along its run. Angi lists a pool line pressure test at $200 to $500.
  • Listening equipmentSensitive microphones pick up the sound of water or air escaping underground, so the technician can mark the spot on the deck or in the yard before cutting.
  • Diving the poolFor cracks low in the shell, at the main drain or in a deep light niche, the technician may get in the water with dye and a mask.

Detection and repair are often separate charges, and sometimes separate companies. Ask before the visit whether the price covers finding the leak only, or finding and fixing it.

Patch, line repair or a bigger job

Once the leak is found, the fix usually falls into one of three groups. A crack in the finish, a loose tile, a skimmer seam or a light niche seal can often be patched or sealed from inside the pool. A broken underground line means opening the ground or deck at the marked spot, replacing the damaged section and testing the line again. And a pool with widespread cracking, failing plaster or a liner at the end of its life may need a resurface or a new liner rather than another patch.

For a vinyl liner pool, "pool lining repair" usually means a patch for a small tear, or a full liner replacement when the vinyl has gone brittle. For a plaster or pebble pool, a patch fixes the spot, but a full replaster is a pool contractor job beyond the R-6 service license.

Good signs in a leak quote

  • It names the leak location and how it was confirmed
  • It separates detection, repair and any deck or yard restoration
  • It explains whether the pool must be partly drained
  • It says how the repair will be tested afterward

Reasons to slow down

  • A big repair is proposed before any test was run
  • Nobody can say where the water is going
  • The plan is to drain the pool to the street
  • No ROC license number is offered when you ask

If a repair does need the water lowered, Tucson and Pima County prefer that pool water go to the sanitary sewer through your home's own cleanout, not the street. The Tucson draining guide has the conditions.

Questions to ask a leak detection or repair company

  • Will you test the equipment pad, the shell and every line, or only part of the system?
  • Does your price cover finding the leak, fixing it, or both?
  • If you need to cut the deck, how big an opening, and who patches it?
  • How will you prove the repair worked: a pressure test, a repeat bucket test?
  • What warranty applies to the repair?
  • What is your ROC license number?
