Catalina Foothills: unincorporated, and right under the mountain

The Catalina Foothills is not a city or a town. The Census Bureau treats it as a census-designated place in Pima County, and the 2020 Census counted 52,401 people there, up from 50,796 in 2010, across nearly 42 square miles of land. With no town government, county departments write the rules on building permits, pool barriers and drainage.

The land shapes those rules. Pima County keeps a separate Catalina Foothills Area Plan in effect, with policies such as preserving Black Wash as riparian habitat, and lots on steep ground fall under the county's Hillside Development Zone, which requires grading plans and slope stabilization details. Above it all sits the Santa Catalina Ranger District of the Coronado National Forest, whose Sabino Canyon Recreation Area draws more than a million visitors a year. The Forest Service tells those hikers to watch for flash floods, a hint at what the canyons can send downhill.

Washes, the Bighorn Fire and foothills pools

In 2020 the Bighorn Fire burned on the front of the Santa Catalina Mountains. Pima County says it raised the risk of flash floods and mudflows in the foothills, because burned soil and lost plants send more water downhill, faster.

Bigger flows than before

The county's flood control district issued post-fire flood maps and property notices for several watersheds, including Esperero. It warns that runoff can be faster and larger than in a typical monsoon storm.

Sediment on the move

Sediment and debris from the burn area made Esperero Wash overtop Sunrise Drive more often, so the county replaced two corrugated metal culverts there with six concrete box culverts. Finger Rock Wash flooded in July 2022 after rain on the burn scar.

What it means in the yard

If your lot sits near a wash, storm water can carry silt, ash and plant debris over a wall and into the pool. Check the county's Bighorn Fire flood risk page for your watershed, and plan on a pool check after a big storm.

Pool service in the Catalina Foothills after the storms

The National Weather Service in Tucson defines monsoon season as June 15 through September 30. Those months bring the heaviest debris loads, and a pool that takes in muddy runoff can turn cloudy or green quickly in summer heat. Regular pool cleaning and maintenance keeps the filter and baskets from choking, a green pool cleanup is the job when the water has already gone, and a pump that ingested grit may need pool pump repair. The Tucson pool maintenance schedule lays out the season month by month.

Safe checks once the storm passes

  • Stay clear of moving water. Never step into a running wash or a flooded yard to reach the pool equipment.
  • Baskets and level. Clear out the skimmer basket and the strainer in front of the pump, and see whether rain pushed the water up.
  • Equipment pad. Look for silt, standing water or a tripped breaker. Reset a tripped breaker or GFCI once; a second trip means leave it off.
  • Keep people out. If you cannot see the bottom, nobody swims, and children stay well away. A murky pool hides anyone who slips under.
  • Mosquitoes. Pima County asks residents to report green, neglected pools that breed mosquitoes to 520-724-7908.

County rules that apply to foothills pools

Barriers

Pima County's page on barriers for residential pools and spas sets the top of the barrier at least 48 inches above grade, measured on the outside, and a bottom edge sitting 2 inches or less above the ground. A 4 inch sphere must not pass through any opening, and pedestrian gates must open outward away from the pool and be self-closing and self-latching. Ask the county's development services before you alter a wall or gate.

Draining and backwash

The discharge guidelines shared by the City of Tucson and Pima County's Regional Wastewater Reclamation Department point pool water to the sanitary sewer first, through the cleanout on your own property, with a hose and a small pump run at low-use hours. Public manholes are off limits. Backwash may go on your own ground only if it soaks in within 24 hours and does not reach a neighbor, a street or an alley. Draining to a street or wash is a last resort with conditions: at least 72 hours since the last chlorine, pH adjusted to between 7 and 8, clear water, never acid-wash water, and no erosion. In a neighborhood laced with washes that feed riparian habitat, the sewer route is the one to plan for. Our draining guide has the full steps.

Water and power by address

Pima County's water resources element names Tucson Water as the provider for the Catalina Foothills planning area. Tucson Water says its hardness rises as the share of Central Arizona Project water in the blend rises, and that hard water is safe to drink; it recommends measuring it with a pool test kit. Because other utilities also operate nearby, check your water bill to see who serves your home. Do the same with your electric bill before setting a pump timer: if it is Tucson Electric Power, its summer on-peak hours on time of use plans are 3 to 7 p.m. on weekdays.
