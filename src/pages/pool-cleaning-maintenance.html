A Tucson pool cleaning service visit, step by step

The order matters. Debris comes off the top before brushing stirs it up, brushing comes before vacuuming so loosened dirt can be picked up, and the water is tested after cleaning so the reading reflects the pool, not the leaves floating on it.

  1. Skim the surface. A leaf net lifts off leaves, palo verde blossoms, bugs and anything else floating, before it sinks and stains the plaster.
  2. Empty the skimmer basket. When that basket is full, too little water reaches the pump, which then strains through the hottest hours.
  3. Clear the pump basket. With the pump switched off, the technician opens the strainer lid, empties the basket and checks the lid seal so the pump does not suck in air.
  4. Brush walls, steps and waterline. Brushing breaks up early algae and dust film in corners and on steps where water moves least.
  5. Vacuum the floor. Fine desert dust settles evenly and hides on light plaster until the pool looks dull; vacuuming takes it out instead of leaving it to cloud the water.
  6. Test the water. Free chlorine, pH and stabilizer at minimum, with calcium hardness and alkalinity on a regular schedule.
  7. Balance the chemistry. The technician adds what the readings call for, one product at a time and never combined, following each label.
  8. Read the filter gauge and look over the equipment. When the needle sits far higher than it did right after the last filter cleaning, dirt is clogging the filter. Drips, odd noises and a wrong timer setting get noted.
  9. Check the water level and leave a note. Low water can let the skimmer draw air. A short visit note tells you what was found and added.

Brushing is the step most often rushed, and the one Tucson pools miss most. Dust that blows in during a summer storm sticks to walls as a thin film. Left alone for two or three weeks, that film becomes a home for algae, and the first green tint often shows on shaded steps and behind ladders.

Vacuuming can be done by hand with a pole and hose, or by an automatic cleaner that runs on its own. An automatic cleaner helps between visits, but it does not brush steps, empty baskets or test the water, so it does not replace the weekly routine.

Pool water chemistry targets, in plain words

These are the readings a careful technician checks. The first two come straight from CDC guidance for home pools; the others depend more on the surface and equipment.

Home pool water chemistry readings, what each one means and the published target where one exists
ReadingWhat it meansPublished target
Free chlorineThe chlorine still available to kill germs.At least 1 ppm; at least 2 ppm when stabilizer is used (CDC)
pHHow acidic or basic the water is. High pH weakens chlorine; low pH is hard on pipes and equipment.7.0 to 7.8 (CDC)
Cyanuric acid (stabilizer)Protects chlorine from sunlight, but also slows how fast it works.No CDC home number; the CDC public pool code caps it at 90 ppm
Calcium hardnessDissolved calcium. Too much leaves white scale on tile and equipment.Ask your technician or plaster maker; public pool codes set a floor near 150 ppm
Total dissolved solidsEverything dissolved in the water, which climbs as water evaporates and is topped off.Watched over time rather than chased weekly

Stabilizer is the reading that confuses most owners. Sunlight breaks chlorine down, and cyanuric acid slows that breakdown, which is why most outdoor pools in the desert carry some. The catch is that it slows chlorine down. The CDC's research shows it delays chlorine's action against tough germs such as Cryptosporidium, and that is why the CDC sets a higher chlorine minimum for stabilized pools.

The CDC also recommends against stabilizer in hot tubs, which matters for Tucson pools with an attached spa. Ask the company how it handles the spa's chemistry.

Why Tucson pool cleaning runs on a weekly schedule

Pools in milder places can drift for a couple of weeks. Here, four pressures land on the water at once.

Sun and heat

National Weather Service normals for 1991 to 2020 give Tucson about 68 days a year at 100 degrees or above, and an average July high near 100. Warm water feeds algae and uses up chlorine, so in summer the chemistry rarely holds much longer than seven days.

Monsoon dust and debris

The monsoon runs June 15 through September 30, according to NWS Tucson. Storm outflow can raise dust storms that cut visibility to almost nothing and leave a gritty layer on the pool floor and in the filter.

Hard water

Tucson Water says its groundwater is naturally hard and that hardness climbs as more Central Arizona Project water is blended in. Calcium shows up as white crust at the waterline; see pool tile cleaning for what happens when it builds up.

Evaporation

The Arizona Department of Water Resources puts evaporation from open water in Tucson at about six feet a year. Water leaves; minerals stay. Each top-off adds more, which is why calcium and dissolved solids creep up over time.

Pool cleaning instructions for owners between visits

The safest homeowner jobs are mechanical. Leave anything involving chemical dosing, acid, wiring or gas to professionals.

Safe for you to do

  • Net leaves and debris off the surface.
  • Tip out the skimmer basket; switch the pump off before you open its strainer basket.
  • Brush the steps and shaded corners.
  • Keep the water level near the middle of the skimmer opening.
  • Test with a home kit and tell the company if a reading looks wrong.
  • Note the filter gauge reading and any drips at the equipment pad.
  • Push the reset on a tripped GFCI or breaker one time only; if it trips again, it stays off until a professional looks.

Leave to a professional

  • Combining any two pool products, ever. The CDC warns that chlorine and acid together can release toxic gas.
  • Acid washing, tile descaling and salt cell cleaning.
  • Opening motor covers, wiring, lights or breaker panels.
  • Any gas heater work. A gas smell means leave the area, touch no switches, and after reaching a safe spot dial 911 and the Southwest Gas emergency number, 877-860-6020.
  • Swimming in murky water, or letting children near it: when you cannot see the floor, you cannot see someone in trouble.

Store whatever products are kept at home the way the CDC advises: in the original labeled containers, dry, locked away from children and pets, out of direct sun and below 95 degrees. In a Tucson garage in July that last point takes real thought.

A one-time cleanup makes sense when you have just bought a house with a neglected pool, a rental sat empty between tenants, a monsoon storm filled the pool with leaves and mud, or a long trip left the water cloudy. It takes longer than a routine visit because the technician starts with unknowns: how loaded the filter is, what the chemistry looks like, and whether anything at the equipment pad has failed.

What to ask a Tucson pool cleaning service

  • Which steps happen on every visit, and which only when needed?
  • Which water readings do you test each week, and which less often?
  • How do you handle stabilizer and calcium creep as the water evaporates?
  • On what schedule do you clean the filter, and does the visit price cover it?
  • What do you do after a dust storm between visits?
  • How will you tell me about a problem with the pump, filter or heater?
  • Which Arizona Registrar of Contractors license do you hold for repair work, and can I see proof of liability insurance?

The ROC's R-6 license covers servicing and minor repairs on residential pools. The ROC does not state whether cleaning alone requires a license, so ask, and look the number up on the ROC contractor search (new tab), which lists license status, classification and recent complaints. The ROC does not require insurance beyond workers' compensation, so ask for the liability certificate directly.
